Keep the training data. To label something new, find the examples it most resembles.

Most models in the book squeeze the training data into a fixed set of parameters \(\boldsymbol\theta\) and then throw the data away. Exemplar-based models (also called instance-based or memory-based) keep every training example and make predictions by comparing a new input to them. The number of things the model "remembers" grows with the dataset, so these are nonparametric models.

Everything here depends on one choice: how to measure distance between two inputs. §16.1 uses a fixed distance and studies the K nearest neighbor classifier. §16.2.2–16.2.4 learn the distance with a neural network. (§16.2.1 on LMNN, NCA and LCA is not part of this reading.)

§16.1

K nearest neighbors: let the closest examples vote

To classify a new input \(\mathbf x\), find the \(K\) training examples closest to it, call that set \(N_K(\mathbf x,\mathcal D)\), and count their labels. The fraction of neighbors with label \(c\) is the predicted probability of class \(c\). You can return that whole distribution, or just the label with the most votes.

The model has two settings you choose: the number of neighbors \(K\), and the distance function \(d(\mathbf x,\mathbf x')\).

Explain itAt your level

You find a leaf on the playground and want to know what tree it came from.

You have a big book of leaves that already have names. You flip through and pick out the 5 leaves that look the most like yours. If 3 of them are maple leaves and 2 are oak leaves, you guess "maple," and you're a little unsure, because 2 of the 5 said oak.

That's the whole idea. You don't learn any rules about leaves. You just keep the book and compare.

Two things matter: how many look-alikes you check (that's K), and what "looks like" means. Do you compare shape? Color? Size? That's the distance.

Plot every training example as a dot on a graph, colored by its label. A new point shows up. Measure its distance to every dot, using the ordinary distance formula \(\sqrt{(\Delta x)^2+(\Delta y)^2}\). Keep the \(K\) closest dots.

If \(K=5\) and the 5 closest dots are red, red, red, blue, blue, then the model says "60% red, 40% blue." If you need one answer, say "red."

There is no training step. All the work happens when a new point arrives, because that's when you measure all the distances.

You can also change how distance is measured. If one direction matters more than another, you can stretch the graph in that direction before measuring. That's what the Mahalanobis option in the Play box does.

Eq. 16.1 is a local frequency estimate: \(p(y=c\mid\mathbf x,\mathcal D)=\frac1K\sum_{n\in N_K(\mathbf x,\mathcal D)}\mathbb I(y_n=c)\). The indicator \(\mathbb I(\cdot)\) is 1 when the statement is true and 0 otherwise, so the sum just counts neighbors with label \(c\).

The usual distance is Mahalanobis (Eq. 16.2): \(d_M(\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\mu)=\sqrt{(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top M(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)}\), with \(M\) positive definite. \(M=I\) gives Euclidean distance. Other choices of \(M\) stretch and rotate space, so the set of points at distance \(r\) becomes an ellipse instead of a circle.

KNN is nonparametric: the "parameters" are the stored data, so model capacity grows with \(N\). Training is free; prediction costs \(O(ND)\) per query if you check every point.

A classic result (Cover and Hart, 1967): as \(N\to\infty\), the error of the 1-NN classifier is at most twice the Bayes error, the error of the best possible classifier. The catch is how fast you approach that limit, which is the topic of §16.1.2.

  • As a density estimate. KNN is a plug-in classifier built from a nonparametric density estimate. Grow a ball around \(\mathbf x\) until it holds \(K\) points; then \(\hat p(\mathbf x\mid c)\propto K_c/(N_c V)\) and \(\hat p(c)=N_c/N\), and Bayes' rule gives \(K_c/K\), which is Eq. 16.1.
  • Cover–Hart bound. For two classes, \(R^*\le R_{1\text{NN}}\le 2R^*(1-R^*)\le 2R^*\) asymptotically. If you let \(K\to\infty\) with \(K/N\to0\), KNN is universally consistent (Stone, 1977): its risk converges to \(R^*\) for any distribution.
  • Rates. Under smoothness assumptions, the excess risk shrinks like \(N^{-c/D}\) for some constant \(c\). The exponent's dependence on \(D\) is the curse of dimensionality in its formal shape.
  • The metric is the model. \(M=W^\top W\) means Euclidean distance after the linear map \(\mathbf z=W\mathbf x\). Learning \(W\), or a nonlinear \(f_\theta\), is the subject of §16.2. Standardizing features is the crude version: a diagonal \(M\) with \(1/\sigma_d^2\) on the diagonal.
  • Practice. Pick \(K\) by cross-validation, weight votes by \(1/d\) or a kernel if you like, and break ties consistently (this page uses the label of the nearest tied neighbor).

Math, slowlyEquations 16.1 – 16.2

The KNN prediction

\[ p(y=c\mid\mathbf x,\mathcal D)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{n\in N_K(\mathbf x,\mathcal D)}\mathbb I(y_n=c) \tag{16.1} \]
  1. \(N_K(\mathbf x,\mathcal D)\) is a set of \(K\) indices: the training examples with the smallest \(d(\mathbf x,\mathbf x_n)\). Sort all \(N\) distances and keep the first \(K\).
  2. \(\mathbb I(y_n=c)\) is 1 if neighbor \(n\) has label \(c\), else 0. The sum counts how many of the \(K\) neighbors are in class \(c\).
  3. Divide by \(K\) to turn the count into a fraction. The fractions over all classes add to 1, so this is a probability distribution.

Worked example (Figure 16.1a). \(K=5\), neighbor labels \(\{1,1,1,0,0\}\):

\[ p(y=1\mid\mathbf x,\mathcal D)=\tfrac15(1+1+1+0+0)=\tfrac35=0.6,\qquad p(y=0\mid\mathbf x,\mathcal D)=\tfrac25=0.4 \]

With \(K=1\), the only possible answers are 0 and 1. The distribution is a spike (a "delta function") on the nearest neighbor's label.

Mahalanobis distance

\[ d_M(\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\mu)=\sqrt{(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top M\,(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)} \tag{16.2} \]

Write \(\boldsymbol\delta=\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu\) and expand in 2-D with \(M=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&c\end{pmatrix}\):

\[ d_M^2=\begin{pmatrix}\delta_1&\delta_2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&c\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\delta_1\\\delta_2\end{pmatrix}=a\,\delta_1^2+2b\,\delta_1\delta_2+c\,\delta_2^2 \]
  1. \(M=I\) means \(a=c=1,\ b=0\), so \(d^2=\delta_1^2+\delta_2^2\). That's Euclidean distance.
  2. A diagonal \(M\) (\(b=0\)) weights each axis separately. \(a=4\) means a step along axis 1 counts twice as far (\(\sqrt4=2\)).
  3. The off-diagonal \(b\) mixes axes, which tilts the ellipse. The Play box builds \(M=R\,\mathrm{diag}(w_1,w_2)\,R^\top\) from a rotation \(R\) and two weights, so you can see this.
  4. "Positive definite" means \(\boldsymbol\delta^\top M\boldsymbol\delta>0\) whenever \(\boldsymbol\delta\neq\mathbf 0\). Distinct points always get a positive distance, and the square root is always defined.
Any positive definite \(M\) can be factored as \(M=W^\top W\). Then \(d_M(\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\mu)=\lVert W\mathbf x-W\boldsymbol\mu\rVert_2\): Mahalanobis distance is Euclidean distance after a linear change of coordinates. §16.2.2 replaces \(W\) with a neural network.

Within a factor of 2 of the best possible

The Bayes error \(R^*\) is the error rate of the best classifier you could build if you knew the true distribution. Cover and Hart showed that, as \(N\to\infty\), the 1-NN error \(R_{1\text{NN}}\) satisfies

\[ R^*\;\le\;R_{1\text{NN}}\;\le\;2R^* \]

Why roughly: with infinite data, your nearest neighbor sits at essentially the same spot as \(\mathbf x\). Its label and the true label of \(\mathbf x\) are two independent draws from the same \(p(y\mid\mathbf x)\). For two classes with \(p=p(y=1\mid\mathbf x)\), they disagree with probability \(2p(1-p)\), and the Bayes error at that spot is \(\min(p,1-p)\). Since \(2p(1-p)\le2\min(p,1-p)\), you get the factor of 2.

§16.1.1

Voronoi cells and choosing K: from memorizing to smoothing

With \(K=1\), each training point owns the region of space closer to it than to any other point. These regions are called Voronoi cells, and the whole partition is a Voronoi tessellation (Figure 16.1b). Every training point lies in its own cell, so the training error is 0. That usually means overfitting.

Larger \(K\) averages over bigger neighborhoods. The decision boundary gets smoother, training error goes up, and test error first falls and then rises: the usual U shape (Figure 16.2).

Explain itAt your level

Imagine every kid on the playground claims the ground that's closer to them than to anyone else.

Draw lines halfway between kids, and the playground splits into little territories, one per kid. If you only ask the one closest kid (K = 1), then whoever owns the territory you're standing in decides the answer.

That works perfectly for the kids themselves, but if one kid is standing in the wrong spot, they make a weird little island. Asking more kids (bigger K) smooths out the islands. Ask too many, though, and you're basically asking the whole school, and the small groups get drowned out.

With \(K=1\), every training point is its own nearest neighbor, so the model gets every training point right. That sounds great, but it means the model also copies every mistake or odd point in the data.

As \(K\) goes up, each prediction is a vote among more points. Odd points get outvoted, and the boundary between colors gets smoother. Training error goes up because the model no longer copies the training data exactly.

The test error (on new data) is what you care about. It's high for tiny \(K\) (too wiggly), low in the middle, and high again for huge \(K\) (too smooth, everything gets the most common label). Pick the \(K\) at the bottom of that U.

The Voronoi cell of \(\mathbf x_n\) is \(V(\mathbf x_n)=\{\mathbf x: d(\mathbf x,\mathbf x_n)

Training error at \(K=1\) is 0 because \(\mathbf x_n\in V(\mathbf x_n)\): when you evaluate on a training point, its nearest neighbor is itself at distance 0.

\(K\) controls model complexity, in the opposite direction from most models: small \(K\) is complex (low bias, high variance), large \(K\) is simple. At \(K=N\) every prediction is the overall majority class. Choose \(K\) with a validation set or cross-validation, not training error.

  • Effective degrees of freedom. A common heuristic (Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman) puts the effective number of parameters at about \(N/K\): roughly \(N/K\) non-overlapping neighborhoods, each fitting one mean.
  • Bias–variance. For KNN regression at a fixed \(\mathbf x\), the variance term is \(\sigma^2/K\) and the bias grows with the radius of the \(K\)-neighborhood. Classification inherits the same tradeoff through 0-1 loss.
  • Evaluating on training data. Training error at \(K=1\) is trivially 0. A fairer in-sample number is leave-one-out error, which excludes the query point from its own neighbors; for KNN it's cheap to compute exactly.
  • Even \(K\) and ties. With two classes, even \(K\) can tie (Figure 16.2b uses \(K=2\)). Use odd \(K\), distance weighting, or a fixed tie-break rule.

Math, slowlyVoronoi cells and the K tradeoff

A Voronoi cell

\[ V(\mathbf x_n)=\{\,\mathbf x:\ d(\mathbf x,\mathbf x_n)
  1. Take two training points \(\mathbf a\) and \(\mathbf b\). The points equally far from both satisfy \(\lVert\mathbf x-\mathbf a\rVert^2=\lVert\mathbf x-\mathbf b\rVert^2\). This is where the 1-NN answer switches from \(\mathbf a\) to \(\mathbf b\).
  2. Expand both sides: \(\lVert\mathbf x\rVert^2-2\mathbf a^\top\mathbf x+\lVert\mathbf a\rVert^2=\lVert\mathbf x\rVert^2-2\mathbf b^\top\mathbf x+\lVert\mathbf b\rVert^2\). The \(\lVert\mathbf x\rVert^2\) terms cancel.
  3. What's left is \(2(\mathbf b-\mathbf a)^\top\mathbf x=\lVert\mathbf b\rVert^2-\lVert\mathbf a\rVert^2\), a straight line (a hyperplane in higher dimensions). It's the perpendicular bisector of \(\mathbf a\) and \(\mathbf b\).
  4. \(V(\mathbf x_n)\) is the intersection of the half-planes "closer to \(\mathbf x_n\) than to \(\mathbf x_m\)" over all \(m\). An intersection of half-planes is a convex polygon, which is why the cells in the Play box have straight edges.

Why training error is 0 at K = 1

Evaluate the classifier at a training point \(\mathbf x_n\). Its distance to itself is \(d(\mathbf x_n,\mathbf x_n)=0\), which is smaller than its distance to any other point (assuming no duplicates). So \(N_1(\mathbf x_n,\mathcal D)=\{n\}\) and the prediction is \(y_n\), which is always right.

What K does

  • \(K=1\): the most flexible model. It follows every training point.
  • \(K=N\): the least flexible. Every query has the same neighbors (all the data), so every prediction is the majority class.
  • In between: test error is usually U-shaped in \(K\). Underfitting is on the right of the curve, overfitting on the left. That's the reverse of plots where the x-axis is "model complexity."

§16.1.2

The curse of dimensionality: in high dimensions, nothing is near

KNN's main statistical problem: it works badly with high-dimensional inputs. Volume grows exponentially with dimension, so to collect even a few neighbors you have to look far away, and far-away points may say little about the label at \(\mathbf x\).

The book's example (from Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman): inputs are uniform in the \(D\)-dimensional unit cube. Grow a small cube around \(\mathbf x\) until it holds a fraction \(p\) of the data. Its edge length is \(e_D(p)=p^{1/D}\). In 10 dimensions, capturing 10% of the data takes a cube whose edge covers 80% of each axis.

Explain itAt your level

Finding a friend on a line is easy. Finding one in a huge building is hard.

If 100 kids stand in a line on the sidewalk, someone is always right next to you. If those same 100 kids spread out over a big field, the closest one might be far away. Now imagine a field that also goes up into the sky, and then more directions that we can't even picture. The kids get so spread out that your "nearest" friend is basically across town.

And if your nearest friend lives across town, asking them "what's the weather here?" isn't very helpful.

Think of the data spread evenly over a square that is 1 unit on each side. To grab 10% of the data, you can use a small square whose area is 0.1. Its side is \(\sqrt{0.1}\approx0.32\), about a third of the way across.

In 3-D, you need a cube with volume 0.1. Its side is \(0.1^{1/3}\approx0.46\). In 10 dimensions, its side is \(0.1^{1/10}\approx0.79\). You're covering 79% of the range of every feature just to get 10% of the data.

So the "neighborhood" isn't local anymore. You're averaging over points that differ from \(\mathbf x\) a lot on most features.

The volume of a cube with edge \(s\) in \(D\) dimensions is \(s^D\). With uniform data on \([0,1]^D\), the expected fraction of points inside is \(s^D\). Setting \(s^D=p\) gives \(e_D(p)=p^{1/D}\), which tends to 1 as \(D\) grows for any fixed \(p\).

Concretely, \(e_{10}(0.1)\approx0.80\) and \(e_{10}(0.01)\approx0.63\). Even 1% of the data needs a neighborhood spanning 63% of each axis.

A related effect (the second Play panel): with many dimensions, distances concentrate. The nearest and farthest points are almost the same distance away, so "nearest" carries little information.

The book's two fixes: assume something about the function (a parametric model), or use a distance that only looks at the dimensions that matter, which is what learned metrics in §16.2 do.

  • Distance concentration. For i.i.d. coordinates, \(\lVert\mathbf x-\mathbf x'\rVert^2\) is a sum of \(D\) i.i.d. terms, so its mean grows like \(D\) and its standard deviation like \(\sqrt D\). The relative spread \(\text{sd}/\text{mean}\to0\), and \((d_{\max}-d_{\min})/d_{\min}\to0\) in probability (Beyer et al., 1999).
  • Sample complexity. To keep a neighborhood of fixed radius \(r\) populated, you need \(N\propto r^{-D}\) points. Nonparametric rates like \(N^{-2/(2+D)}\) (smooth regression) make this formal.
  • Why KNN still works on images and text. Real data lies near a low-dimensional manifold. What matters is the intrinsic dimension, not \(D\). Learned embeddings (§16.2.2) try to make Euclidean distance follow that manifold.
  • Hubness. In high \(D\), a few points end up in the \(K\)-NN lists of many queries ("hubs"), which skews KNN votes and retrieval (Radovanović et al., 2010).

Math, slowlyThe edge-length formula

  1. A cube with edge \(s\) in \(D\) dimensions has volume \(s\cdot s\cdots s=s^D\). A square has area \(s^2\), a cube \(s^3\), and so on.
  2. The data are uniform on the unit cube, which has volume 1. So the expected fraction of points inside the small cube equals its volume, \(s^D\). Uniform means "probability = volume."
  3. We want that fraction to be \(p\): set \(s^D=p\). This defines how big the neighborhood must be.
  4. Take the \(D\)-th root of both sides. This gives the formula.
\[ e_D(p)\triangleq p^{1/D} \]
Dp = 0.01p = 0.1p = 0.5

For any fixed \(p\) between 0 and 1, \(p^{1/D}\to p^0=1\) as \(D\to\infty\). The neighborhood ends up spanning the whole range of every feature.

Why distances bunch up (the second panel)

For two independent uniform points, each coordinate difference \(\Delta_d\) has \(\mathbb E[\Delta_d^2]=\tfrac16\). The squared distance is a sum of \(D\) such terms:

\[ \lVert\mathbf x-\mathbf x'\rVert^2=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\Delta_d^2,\qquad \mathbb E\big[\lVert\mathbf x-\mathbf x'\rVert^2\big]=\frac D6,\qquad \mathrm{sd}\propto\sqrt D \]

The spread relative to the mean shrinks like \(\sqrt D/D=1/\sqrt D\). So in high dimensions every point is about the same distance away.

§16.1.3

Reducing speed and memory: don't check every point

KNN stores all the training data, which wastes memory. Pruning methods remove points that don't affect the decision boundary (§17.4 covers a more principled version, sparse kernel machines).

The bigger issue is time. Checking every training point costs \(O(N)\) per query. Exact search gets intractable above about 10 dimensions, so most methods look for approximate nearest neighbors. There are two families: partition space (k-d trees, or clustering around anchor points) and hashing (locality sensitive hashing, LSH). FAISS is a widely used open-source library for exact and approximate search over dense vectors.

Explain itAt your level

If you're looking for a book, you don't read every book in the library.

The library is split into sections, and sections into shelves. You walk to the right shelf and only look there. A k-d tree does that for points: it keeps cutting the space in half, so you can skip big chunks that are too far away.

Hashing is like sorting kids into teams by asking a few yes/no questions: "Are you left of the tree? Are you above the fence?" Kids with the same answers are probably close together, so you only check your own team. Sometimes your true closest friend ended up on another team, and you miss them. That's the price for being fast.

Brute force: measure the distance to all \(N\) points. With a million points, that's a million distance calculations for every single prediction.

A k-d tree splits the data at the median along \(x\), then each half at the median along \(y\), and so on. To search, go down to the region holding the query, then only back up into other regions if they could contain something closer. In 2-D this often checks a few dozen points instead of hundreds.

LSH draws random lines. Each point gets a code: which side of line 1, which side of line 2, and so on. Points close together usually get the same code. You only compare against points with your code. More lines means smaller groups (faster) but a bigger chance of missing the true nearest neighbor.

Costs: memory \(O(ND)\); brute-force query \(O(ND)\). A k-d tree is built in \(O(N\log N)\) and in low dimensions answers queries in about \(O(\log N)\) on average. It uses branch and bound: skip a subtree if the distance from the query to its splitting plane is larger than the best distance found so far.

In high dimensions that pruning test almost never fires (distances concentrate, §16.1.2), so the search visits most of the tree. That's the reason exact search is hopeless above ~10 dimensions. Run the "k-d tree vs dimension" experiment below to see it.

LSH with random hyperplanes: \(h(\mathbf x)=\mathrm{sign}(\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x+b)\) with random \(\mathbf w\). Close points are likely to land on the same side of each hyperplane. Concatenate \(L\) bits into a bucket code and search only your bucket. Use several hash tables to catch neighbors that one table misses.

  • LSH guarantees. A family is \((r,cr,p_1,p_2)\)-sensitive if close pairs collide with probability \(\ge p_1\) and far pairs with \(\le p_2\). With \(L\) bits per table and \(T\) tables, you get sublinear query time \(O(N^\rho)\), \(\rho=\log(1/p_1)/\log(1/p_2)<1\), for \(c\)-approximate NN (Indyk and Motwani, 1998). For random hyperplanes through the origin, \(\Pr[h(\mathbf x)=h(\mathbf y)]=1-\theta(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)/\pi\) (Charikar, 2002).
  • Learned hashing and quantization. Modern systems learn the codes from data, or use product quantization (compress vectors into short codes and compute approximate distances from lookup tables). FAISS combines inverted-file indexes (k-means cells, the "anchor point" approach) with PQ, and also supports graph indexes like HNSW.
  • Exactness tradeoffs. Benchmarks report recall@K vs queries per second. Most production retrieval is approximate, with recall in the 0.9–0.99 range.
  • Memory. Condensed NN (Hart, 1968) and editing methods keep only points near the boundary. Sparse kernel machines (§17.4) do this with a sparsity-promoting prior.

Math, slowlyCosts and the two shortcuts

Brute force

One distance in \(D\) dimensions costs \(O(D)\). A query checks all \(N\) points, so \(O(ND)\) per query, and storing the data takes \(O(ND)\) memory.

k-d tree pruning rule

  1. Each internal node splits on one coordinate \(j\) at value \(t\): left if \(x_jAxis-parallel cuts, cycling through coordinates.
  2. Search the side that contains the query first, and keep the best distance found so far, \(d_{\text{best}}\).
  3. Every point on the other side differs from the query by at least \(|q_j-t|\) along coordinate \(j\), so its distance is at least \(|q_j-t|\). One coordinate's difference is a lower bound on Euclidean distance.
  4. If \(|q_j-t|\ge d_{\text{best}}\), nothing on the other side can win, so skip it. That's the "bound" in branch and bound.

In high \(D\), \(d_{\text{best}}\) is large compared with the gap to any single splitting plane, so step 4 rarely skips anything.

Random-hyperplane hashing

\[ h_\ell(\mathbf x)=\mathrm{sign}(\mathbf w_\ell^\top\mathbf x+b_\ell),\qquad \text{code}(\mathbf x)=\big(h_1(\mathbf x),\dots,h_L(\mathbf x)\big) \]

For hyperplanes through the origin (\(b=0\)) with random direction \(\mathbf w\), two vectors at angle \(\theta\) land on the same side with probability \(1-\theta/\pi\). With \(L\) independent bits, they share a full code with probability \((1-\theta/\pi)^L\). Close vectors (small \(\theta\)) still collide often; far ones rarely do. Larger \(L\) shrinks buckets but also lowers the collision chance for true neighbors, which is the recall drop you see in the Play box.

§16.1.4

Open set recognition: "none of the above" is an answer

So far the set of classes has been fixed (the closed world assumption). Real systems meet inputs from classes they've never seen. A face recognizer trained on a gallery of known people will eventually see a stranger, or a photo of a dog.

KNN handles this naturally. Store the labeled examples in a gallery. For a new input, find the nearest gallery example \(\mathbf x'\). If it's close enough, it's a match (face verification, person re-identification). If not, declare the input novel (a new person: novelty detection) or out-of-distribution (not a face at all: OOD detection). A novel input can be added to the gallery with a new label, which is incremental (online, continual, life-long) learning. With only one or a few examples per class, this is few-shot classification.

Explain itAt your level

A school guard knows every kid's face. A new face walks up. What should the guard say?

If the face looks a lot like Ana, the guard says "Hi, Ana." But if it doesn't look much like anyone, the guard shouldn't just pick the closest kid and say "Hi, Ben." The right answer is "I don't know you. What's your name?"

Then the guard remembers the new kid's face, so tomorrow they're known too. That's learning one new person at a time.

Plain KNN always answers with one of the labels it knows, even for a picture of a dog. To fix that, add a rule: if the nearest stored example is farther away than some threshold \(\tau\), answer "unknown."

Choosing \(\tau\) is a tradeoff. A small \(\tau\) rejects strangers but also rejects known people whose photo is a bit off. A large \(\tau\) accepts known people easily but also mistakes strangers for someone it knows.

When something is unknown, the system can ask for its label and store it. Now the class list has grown by one, with no retraining.

Setup: at time \(t\) the dataset is \(\mathcal D_t=\{(\mathbf x_n,y_n)\}\) with labels in \(\mathcal C_t=\{1,\dots,C_t\}\). A new input \(\mathbf x_{t+1}\) might come from a known class, from a new class \(C_{t+1}\notin\mathcal C_t\), or from an unrelated distribution \(p'(\mathbf x)\).

Nearest-exemplar rule: \(n^*=\arg\min_n d(\mathbf x_{t+1},\mathbf x_n)\). If \(d(\mathbf x_{t+1},\mathbf x_{n^*})\le\tau\), predict \(y_{n^*}\); otherwise flag it as novel/OOD. If labeled, set \(\mathcal D_{t+1}=\mathcal D_t\cup\{(\mathbf x_{t+1},C_{t+1})\}\) and \(\mathcal C_{t+1}=\mathcal C_t\cup\{C_{t+1}\}\).

A parametric softmax classifier has a fixed output layer with \(C_t\) units, so adding a class means changing the model. A gallery just gets one more row. The hard part is the distance: a new person who looks like a known one is hard to flag. That's why §16.2 learns the metric.

Related open-world problems (§16.1.4.2): entity resolution ("John Smith" vs "Jon Smith": same entity?) and multi-object tracking (is a new radar blip an existing target or a new one?).

  • Verification vs identification. Face systems report TAR at fixed FAR (true accept rate at a false accept rate like \(10^{-4}\)). The threshold \(\tau\) sets the operating point on that ROC-like curve; the Play box draws the two error rates against \(\tau\).
  • Calibrating \(\tau\). A global threshold assumes every class is equally tight. Per-class thresholds, or scoring by the ratio of first to second nearest distance (Lowe's ratio test), often work better.
  • Softmax confidence is a weak OOD detector. Networks can be confidently wrong far from the data. Distance-based scores in feature space (KNN distance, Mahalanobis on penultimate features) are strong baselines (Lee et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2022).
  • Continual learning. A growing gallery avoids catastrophic forgetting in the classifier, but the embedding network itself can still drift if you keep fine-tuning it.
  • Random finite sets (Mahler; Vo et al.) model a variable, unknown number of objects with set-valued random variables, for tracking problems.

§16.2.2

Deep metric learning: measure distance in a learned embedding

For images, audio or text, Euclidean distance on raw inputs is nearly meaningless: two photos of the same person can differ in almost every pixel. Instead, learn an embedding \(\mathbf e=f(\mathbf x;\boldsymbol\theta)\in\mathbb R^L\), a lower-dimensional vector that keeps the "semantic" content, and measure distance there. When \(f\) is a deep network, this is deep metric learning (DML).

The embedding is usually scaled to length 1, \(\hat{\mathbf e}=\mathbf e/\lVert\mathbf e\rVert_2\), so all points lie on a sphere. Then distance and cosine similarity are two views of the same number.

Explain itAt your level

Two photos of your dog, one in the sun and one in the shade, look really different pixel by pixel. But they're the same dog.

So we teach a computer to describe each picture with a short list of numbers, like a secret code, where "same dog" gives nearly the same code and "different dog" gives a different code. Then we compare the codes instead of the pictures.

We also make every code the same "size." Then the only thing that matters is which way the code points, like arrows on a compass. Two arrows pointing the same way are a match.

A neural network turns each input into a short list of numbers, an arrow in space. It's trained so that similar inputs give arrows pointing in similar directions.

Then each arrow is shrunk or stretched to length 1. Now every arrow ends on a circle (or a sphere, in more dimensions), and what matters is the angle between two arrows.

Two ways to compare: the straight-line distance between the arrow tips (small = similar), or the cosine of the angle (big = similar; 1 means same direction). For length-1 arrows, these always agree: distance² = 2 − 2 × cosine. Try it in the Play box.

Training needs pairs labeled "similar" or "not similar." Class labels give you that for free: same label means similar. Sections 16.2.3–16.2.4 are about how to train the network.

Normalized Euclidean distance (Eq. 16.7): \(d(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf x_j;\boldsymbol\theta)=\lVert\hat{\mathbf e}_i-\hat{\mathbf e}_j\rVert_2^2\), smaller is more similar. Cosine similarity (Eq. 16.8): \(\hat{\mathbf e}_i^\top\hat{\mathbf e}_j\), larger is more similar. Because \(\lVert\hat{\mathbf e}\rVert=1\), expanding the square gives Eq. 16.9: \(\lVert\hat{\mathbf e}_i-\hat{\mathbf e}_j\rVert_2^2=2-2\,\hat{\mathbf e}_i^\top\hat{\mathbf e}_j\). Ranking by one is the same as ranking by the other.

From labels \(\mathcal D=\{(\mathbf x_i,y_i)\}\) you get similar pairs \(\mathcal S=\{(i,j):y_i=y_j\}\). Training should make \((i,j)\in\mathcal S\) close and \((i,k)\notin\mathcal S\) far. Labels are optional: self-supervised methods (§19.2.4) build similar pairs automatically, for example two random crops of the same image.

The book's warning: many newer DML papers overstate their gains because of unfair experimental comparisons (Musgrave et al., 2020; Roth et al., 2020). The book focuses on older, simpler losses that hold up.

  • Why normalize. Without it, the network can lower a margin-based loss just by scaling all embeddings up or down. On the sphere, the scale is fixed, the loss depends only on angles, and the temperature in §16.2.4.3 becomes the explicit scale knob.
  • Geometry. On \(S^{L-1}\), chord distance \(\sqrt{2-2\cos\theta}=2\sin(\theta/2)\) is monotone in geodesic angle \(\theta\), so it's a true metric that agrees with angular distance on rankings.
  • Retrieval at scale. Unit-norm embeddings let you use maximum inner product search, which FAISS and similar tools are built for (§16.1.3).
  • The benchmarking critique. Musgrave et al. found that with matched backbones, embedding sizes, augmentation and hyperparameter search, contrastive and triplet baselines from 2006–2015 come close to most post-2016 losses. Report mean ± std over seeds with a held-out validation split.

§16.2.3

Classification losses: train a classifier, keep its features

The simplest way to get an embedding: if you have labeled data with \(C\) classes, train an ordinary classifier (cost \(O(NC)\)) and use one of its hidden layers as \(f(\mathbf x)\). People usually take the second-to-last layer, since it carries over to new classes better than the final layer.

The drawback: a classifier only needs each example on the correct side of a decision boundary. That doesn't force same-class examples to be close or different-class examples to be far. And you can't use this method without labels.

Explain itAt your level

A sorting game: put every toy in the right bin.

A kid who learns this game learns where the walls between bins are. They don't care if a toy car sits right next to the wall or way in the back of its bin, as long as it's in the right bin.

But then if you ask "which toy is most like this car?", the kid might point at a truck on the other side of the wall, because it's physically closer than another car way in the back of the car bin. Knowing the walls isn't the same as knowing what's similar.

A classifier's job is to put each example in the right class. Training stops caring once an example is clearly on the right side of the line.

If you take the numbers the classifier computes just before its final answer and use them as an embedding, same-class points can end up spread along a long strip, and points from two different classes can sit close together near the line between them.

That's fine for classifying, but bad for nearest-neighbor search: the closest point might be from the wrong class. The Play box shows two embeddings with the same classification accuracy and very different nearest-neighbor accuracy.

With a linear softmax head, \(p(y=c\mid\mathbf x)\propto\exp(\mathbf w_c^\top\mathbf e)\). Cross-entropy is minimized by making \(\mathbf w_{y}^\top\mathbf e\) larger than the other logits. Scaling \(\mathbf e\) up makes the margins bigger without moving any point closer to its classmates. So cross-entropy rewards points that are far out along their class's direction, which tends to produce long, spread-out "spokes" rather than tight clusters.

The penultimate layer is preferred over the logits because the logits are tied to exactly the training classes; earlier features are more general and transfer better to classes the network never saw (the few-shot setting of §16.1.4).

Pros: simple, scales to many examples, uses standard training code. Cons: needs labels; no direct pressure for small within-class distances.

  • Radial features. Softmax features are known to spread radially; norm grows with confidence. L2-normalizing the features (and weights) at test time, or during training, removes this and helps retrieval.
  • Normalized softmax variants. NormFace, CosFace, ArcFace put features and class weights on the sphere and add an angular margin. They are classification losses that behave like metric losses, and are strong face-recognition baselines. (These aren't in the book's text; they're the modern answer to this section's drawback.)
  • Proxy view. Each \(\mathbf w_c\) acts as a learned "proxy" exemplar for class \(c\), so the loss is like an N-pairs loss (§16.2.4.3) against \(C\) proxies, costing \(O(NC)\) instead of \(O(N^2)\).
  • Transfer. Features from the last layers specialize to the training label set (neural collapse: within-class variability shrinks toward class means late in training, Papyan et al. 2020). Earlier layers keep more information for new classes.

§16.2.4.1

Contrastive loss and Siamese networks: pull pairs together, push others past a margin

Ranking losses (§16.2.4) directly ask that similar examples be closer than dissimilar ones. Most of them don't need class labels, only a way to say which pairs are similar.

The earliest is the contrastive loss (Chopra, Hadsell and LeCun, 2005). For a positive pair (same label), the loss is the squared distance. For a negative pair, the loss is zero once they're at least a margin \(m\) apart, and grows the closer they get. Both inputs go through the same network \(f(\cdot;\boldsymbol\theta)\), which is called a Siamese network.

Explain itAt your level

Two magnets rules, with a fence.

If two pictures show the same thing, pull them together, always. If they show different things, push them apart, but only until they're at least a certain distance apart (the fence). After that, leave them alone.

The same "picture-to-code machine" looks at both pictures. That's why it's called Siamese, like twins: two copies of the very same machine.

Take two examples. Run each through the same network to get two embeddings. Measure the distance \(d\) between them.

  • Same class: loss = \(d^2\). Any distance costs something, so training pulls them together.
  • Different class: loss = \((m-d)^2\) if \(d

Why the margin? Without it, the network could make the loss as small as it wants by pushing negatives infinitely far. With it, "far enough" is enough.

  • Scale mismatch. Positive and negative terms are optimized independently on absolute scales: positives toward 0, negatives toward \(m\). Their magnitudes aren't comparable, and a single global \(m\) may suit some classes and not others. This is the book's stated reason for moving to triplets.
  • Variants. Some versions put a positive margin too, \([d-m_+]_+^2\), so positives aren't pushed to collapse onto a single point.
  • Mining. With random pairs, most negatives are already beyond \(m\). Hard-negative mining (picking close negatives) makes training efficient but can be unstable early on.
  • Historical use. Signature verification (Bromley et al., 1993) and face verification (Chopra et al., 2005); DrLIM (Hadsell et al., 2006) used it for dimensionality reduction.

§16.2.4.2

Triplet loss: the positive must be closer than the negative, by a margin

In the contrastive loss, positive pairs and negative pairs are handled separately, so their sizes aren't comparable. The triplet loss (Schroff, Kalenichenko and Philbin, 2015) compares them directly. For each anchor \(\mathbf x_i\), pick a positive \(\mathbf x_i^+\) (similar) and a negative \(\mathbf x_i^-\) (dissimilar). The loss is zero when the anchor-to-positive distance is smaller than the anchor-to-negative distance by at least \(m\).

Naively there are \(O(N^3)\) triplets. In practice you compute the loss on minibatches built to contain at least one positive and one negative for each anchor.

Explain itAt your level

Three photos: you, another photo of you, and your friend.

The rule: the two photos of you must be closer together than you and your friend, with some extra room to spare. If that's already true, nothing needs to change. If not, scoot your two photos together and scoot your friend away.

The rule doesn't care exactly how close or far things are. It only cares about "this one is closer than that one."

Measure two distances from the anchor: to the positive (\(d_+\)) and to the negative (\(d_-\)). We want \(d_+^2+m\le d_-^2\).

The loss is \(\max(0,\ d_+^2-d_-^2+m)\). If the negative is already far enough, the loss is 0. Otherwise the loss tells you how much the rule is broken by.

In the Play box, the dashed circle around the anchor marks "far enough." Drag the negative outside the dashed circle and the loss drops to 0.

Negatives come in three kinds: easy (outside the dashed circle, loss 0), semi-hard (between the circles), and hard (closer than the positive).

Eq. 16.11: \(\mathcal L(\boldsymbol\theta;\mathbf x_i,\mathbf x_i^+,\mathbf x_i^-)=[d_\theta(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf x_i^+)^2-d_\theta(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf x_i^-)^2+m]_+\), averaged over triplets.

With squared Euclidean distance on embeddings \(\mathbf a,\mathbf p,\mathbf n\), when the hinge is active the gradients are \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\mathbf p=2(\mathbf p-\mathbf a)\), \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\mathbf n=-2(\mathbf n-\mathbf a)\), \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\mathbf a=2(\mathbf n-\mathbf p)\). A descent step pulls \(\mathbf p\) toward \(\mathbf a\), pushes \(\mathbf n\) away, and moves \(\mathbf a\) toward \(\mathbf p\) and away from \(\mathbf n\). In a real network these flow back into \(\boldsymbol\theta\) through the three copies of \(f\) (Figure 16.5b).

Because the loss is relative, a class can be spread out as long as negatives are farther still. That's the advantage over the contrastive loss.

  • Mining is everything. Most random triplets are easy and give zero gradient. FaceNet used online semi-hard mining within large batches; batch-hard mining (Hermans et al., 2017) takes the hardest positive and negative per anchor in the batch.
  • Hard negatives can collapse training. Choosing the very hardest negatives early can drive all embeddings to one point (\(f\equiv\)const gives loss exactly \(m\) for every triplet, a stable bad equilibrium). Semi-hard negatives avoid this.
  • Margin and normalization. On the unit sphere squared distances lie in \([0,4]\), so \(m\) is interpretable (FaceNet used 0.2). Without normalization \(m\) can be undone by rescaling.
  • Soft-margin variant. Replace the hinge with softplus, \(\log(1+e^{z})\), which never gives exactly zero gradient. With \(N=2\) the N-pairs loss is this idea on cosine similarities (§16.2.4.3).

§16.2.4.3

N-pairs loss: pick the positive out of a lineup

The triplet loss compares each anchor with only one negative at a time, which may be a weak learning signal. The N-pairs loss (Sohn, 2016) uses one positive and \(N-1\) negatives at once and turns the problem into an \(N\)-way classification: "which of these is the positive?" The score for each candidate is its cosine similarity with the anchor, and the loss is softmax cross-entropy.

This is the same as the InfoNCE loss from contrastive predictive coding. Dividing the similarities by a temperature gives the NT-Xent loss used in SimCLR. With \(N=2\) it reduces to the logistic loss, a smooth version of the triplet hinge.

Explain itAt your level

A police lineup, but for pictures.

You see a photo of a cat. Then you see a row of pictures: one is another photo of the same cat, the rest are other animals. Can you point to the right one?

The computer plays this game over and over. Each time it points to the wrong picture, or isn't sure, it adjusts so that next time the matching picture stands out more from the crowd. Playing with a long row of pictures teaches it more than playing with just two.

Score every candidate by how similar it is to the anchor (the cosine, from −1 to 1). Turn the scores into chances that add to 100% using the softmax: raise \(e\) to each score and divide by the total.

The loss is \(-\log\)(chance given to the true positive). If the positive gets 90%, the loss is small (0.11). If it gets 10%, the loss is big (2.3).

Compared with the triplet loss, each update looks at many negatives, so the anchor learns to stand apart from all of them at once.

The temperature divides every score before the softmax. A small temperature exaggerates differences: the closest negative dominates. A large one flattens everything.

Eq. 16.13: \(\mathcal L=-\log\frac{\exp(\hat{\mathbf e}^\top\hat{\mathbf e}^+)}{\exp(\hat{\mathbf e}^\top\hat{\mathbf e}^+)+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\exp(\hat{\mathbf e}^\top\hat{\mathbf e}_k^-)}\). That's cross-entropy for an \(N\)-way softmax whose logits are the cosine similarities and whose correct class is the positive.

Divide the top and bottom by \(\exp(\hat{\mathbf e}^\top\hat{\mathbf e}^+)\) to get Eq. 16.12: \(\log\big(1+\sum_k\exp(s_k^--s^+)\big)\). Each negative contributes according to how close its similarity is to the positive's.

With \(N=2\) (one negative): \(\log(1+\exp(s^--s^+))\), Eq. 16.14, the logistic (softplus) loss. The triplet-style hinge with \(m=1\) is \(\max(0,\ s^--s^++1)\), Eq. 16.15. Both are functions of the same difference \(z=s^--s^+\); the logistic one is smooth and never exactly zero. Compare them in the second Play panel.

NT-Xent replaces \(s\) by \(s/\tau\). The book's interpretation: \(\tau\) scales the radius of the hypersphere the embeddings live on.

  • Gradient weighting. \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial s_k^-=p_k\) and \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial s^+=p^+-1\), where \(p\) is the softmax over candidates. Negatives are weighted by their softmax probability, so hard negatives get more gradient automatically: built-in soft mining, sharpened by low \(\tau\).
  • Mutual information. InfoNCE with \(N\) candidates gives \(I(\mathbf x;\mathbf x^+)\ge\log N-\mathcal L\) (van den Oord et al., 2018). The bound saturates at \(\log N\), one reason SimCLR-style methods want large batches.
  • Efficiency. In a batch of \(B\) pairs, each anchor uses the other \(B-1\) positives as negatives, giving \(B(B-1)\) negative comparisons for \(2B\) forward passes. Sohn's main practical contribution was this batch construction.
  • Temperature. Low \(\tau\) emphasizes hard negatives and gives more uniform embeddings but is less tolerant of false negatives (same-class items treated as negatives); Wang and Liu (2021) analyze this tradeoff. Typical \(\tau\) values are 0.05–0.5.

Math, slowlyEquations 16.12 – 16.15

Shorthand: \(s^+=\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta(\mathbf x)^\top\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta(\mathbf x^+)\) and \(s_k^-=\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta(\mathbf x)^\top\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta(\mathbf x_k^-)\), the cosine similarities of the anchor with the positive and with negative \(k\).

Start from the softmax form

\[ \mathcal L=-\log\frac{\exp(s^+)}{\exp(s^+)+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\exp(s_k^-)} \tag{16.13} \]

This is cross-entropy where the "classes" are the \(N\) candidates and the right answer is the positive.

Get the other form

  1. Divide the top and bottom of the fraction by \(\exp(s^+)\): \(\dfrac{1}{1+\sum_k\exp(s_k^-)/\exp(s^+)}\). This doesn't change the value.
  2. Use \(e^a/e^b=e^{a-b}\): the bottom is \(1+\sum_k\exp(s_k^--s^+)\).
  3. \(-\log(1/u)=\log u\). That flips the sign.
\[ \mathcal L=\log\Big(1+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\exp\big(s_k^--s^+\big)\Big) \tag{16.12} \]

Each negative adds \(\exp(s_k^--s^+)\). A negative much less similar than the positive adds almost nothing; one more similar adds more than 1.

Temperature (NT-Xent)

\[ \mathcal L_\tau=-\log\frac{\exp(s^+/\tau)}{\exp(s^+/\tau)+\sum_k\exp(s_k^-/\tau)} \]

Dividing by \(\tau\) is the same as using embeddings of length \(1/\sqrt\tau\) instead of 1, since \((\mathbf u/\sqrt\tau)^\top(\mathbf v/\sqrt\tau)=\mathbf u^\top\mathbf v/\tau\). That's the "radius of the hypersphere" interpretation.

N = 2 and the comparison with the hinge

\[ \mathcal L(\boldsymbol\theta;\mathbf x,\mathbf x^+,\mathbf x^-)=\log\big(1+\exp(s^--s^+)\big) \tag{16.14} \]
\[ \mathcal L(\boldsymbol\theta;\mathbf x,\mathbf x^+,\mathbf x^-)=\max\big(0,\ s^--s^++1\big) \tag{16.15} \]

Let \(z=s^--s^+\). Then 16.14 is \(\log(1+e^z)\) and 16.15 is \(\max(0,z+1)\). For very negative \(z\) both go to 0; for large \(z\) both grow like \(z\) (the hinge as \(z+1\), the logistic as about \(z\)). The difference is near the corner: the hinge is exactly 0 for \(z\le-1\) and stops learning, while the logistic loss keeps a small gradient everywhere. These are the same two curves as in the book's Figure 4.2.

Explain itAt your level

Same toys, three different tidy-up rules.

Rule 1: "Pull matching toys together; push different toys apart until they're one step away." Rule 2: "Each toy should be closer to a matching toy than to a different toy." Rule 3: "Each toy should be able to pick out its match from a crowd." Watch how each rule tidies the room.

All three rules group same-colored dots together, but the final pictures look different. The contrastive loss squeezes each color into a tight ball, because it pulls same-class pairs together no matter what. The triplet loss stops as soon as each point is closer to its own color than to others by the margin, so groups may stay loose. The N-pairs loss lives on a circle and spreads the groups out evenly around it.

Watch the loss curve and the 1-NN accuracy. All three should end with every point's nearest neighbor in its own class.

Full-batch gradients over all \(\binom{30}{2}=435\) pairs (contrastive), all valid triplets (triplet: each anchor, each positive, each negative), or each anchor with every positive and all negatives (N-pairs with the softmax over the anchor's positive plus all 20 negatives, on normalized points). Each point is a free parameter, so this is like a network with unlimited capacity: the only thing shaping the result is the loss.

Things to notice: contrastive collapses each class toward a point (the \(d^2\) term has no floor); triplet stops at zero loss once every margin holds, leaving classes partly spread; N-pairs, being on the circle with a softmax, pushes classes to be roughly evenly spaced in angle.

  • Alignment and uniformity. Wang and Isola (2020) decompose contrastive/InfoNCE objectives into an alignment term (positives close) and a uniformity term (embeddings spread over the sphere). The N-pairs run shows both: classes tighten and spread evenly around the circle.
  • Collapse. Contrastive without a positive margin drives within-class variance to zero. That's harmless here and can hurt transfer to unseen classes, since within-class variation sometimes carries information.
  • Real training differs: \(f_\theta\) has limited capacity and shares parameters across inputs, losses are computed on minibatches with mining, and generalization to held-out classes is what's measured (Recall@K on unseen classes, as in CUB-200 / Cars196 / SOP benchmarks).
